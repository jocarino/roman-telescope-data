# Review — 14 Educators, planetariums & outreach

*Reviewed by a secondary physics teacher (20 years, state comprehensive) and a planetarium education officer who books outreach content. Where we disagree, we say which of us is talking.*

## Verdict

**Strong doc, one genuinely excellent idea buried at rank 3, and two curriculum claims that would have cost you the audience.** The plan is unusually honest about effort, but it proposes six artefacts when one — the anchor lesson — is the only thing here I would actually put in front of a class.

## As a teacher: would I use this?

**1. `/teach` page.** *Yes, and build it first.* Not because it teaches anything — it doesn't — but because it is where I check three things before I commit a period: can my kids get on it without a login, can I print a fallback, and am I allowed to photocopy it. Answer those three above the fold, in that order, and I'll trust the rest. One change: put the **licence and the "works on a school laptop, no account" line at the top**, not in a footer. That is the sentence that gets a resource past a head of department.

**2. "Why is this one blue?" worksheet.** *No — not as specified.* "Pick three planets from a named list, read the spectrum plot, predict the colour" fails in period 5 for a specific reason: **predict-then-check with an instant on-screen answer is not a prediction, it's a race.** Thirty teenagers will click the swatch first and reverse-engineer the reasoning, and I cannot tell from their books which ones understood anything. It also assumes students can read an absorption dip on an unfamiliar axis with no scaffolding — that is a whole lesson on its own, not a warm-up. And 45 minutes is 45 minutes of *timetable*; I lose 6–8 to settling, register and packing up. The real usable block is 35. If you keep it, the fix is: **the answer must not be visible on the same screen as the question.** Give them the spectrum on paper and the colour on the site — or vice versa.

**3. Solar-system anchor activity.** *Yes. This is the one.* It is the only proposal here that produces something I can mark, targets a misconception I actually fight every year, and works with the wifi down. Fully developed below — because as written in the doc it is two sentences and a time box, which is not a lesson.

**4. No-internet PDF fallback.** *Yes, but it's mis-scoped.* A 20-planet printable pack is a nice-to-have. What I need is **one printable page per activity**, not a pack. A 20-page PDF is a 20-page photocopy bill, and no department will approve it. Build the fallback as a by-product of the worksheet (a single A4 with the five anchor pairs on it), and ship the 20-planet pack later, if ever.

**5. Printable poster.** *Yes — and rank it higher than 5.* Posters do teach, indirectly: I have a Hertzsprung–Russell diagram on my wall that I have pointed at in maybe four hundred lessons. But make it **A3 and A4-tiling only**. A0 requires a print budget and a laminator booking, which means it never happens. A3 goes on the wall the same afternoon. Put the QR code where a kid at the back can photograph it.

**6. Guided tour as a lesson starter.** *Yes, and it is already built.* `start-here — "Start here: five worlds we can check"` is exactly this. It needs a time box and two sentences of teacher note, not a weekend. Move it to rank 2 on effort alone.

**7. Portuguese translations.** *Yes, and non-negotiable for the ESERO route.* A Portuguese teacher will not adopt an English worksheet for a 10.º-ano class, however good it is.

**The honest summary:** you have proposed six artefacts and I would use two of them. Building all six is the failure mode the doc's own Risks section names — and then does not act on.

## The model-vs-observation lesson

**Title:** *Does the model agree with the photograph?*
**Level:** 14–16 (KS4 / 10.º ano), and runs unmodified as A-level enrichment.
**Time:** one 50-minute period. Real usable time 40 min; timings below are the usable ones.
**Prior knowledge assumed:** white light is a mixture of wavelengths; materials absorb some wavelengths and reflect others. **That is all.** No spectroscopy maths, no astronomy. Deliberately: the moment a resource assumes the Bohr model, half my classes are out.
**Kit:** one A4 sheet per student. Laptops optional — the lesson works fully on paper. If the wifi is up, one shared screen is enough.

**Objective (write it on the board):** *Explain how you decide whether a scientific model is wrong, or the measurement it disagrees with is.*

**Why this content and not another:** every curriculum asks students to evaluate models against evidence, and it is nearly always taught with an example where the model is simply wrong and the data is simply right. That teaches the wrong lesson. Here, four cases agree and **one disagrees because the photograph is the misleading one** — Voyager 2's famous deep-blue Neptune was contrast-enhanced in processing, and the computed colour is the one that matches modern reanalysis. I have never had a better example of this, and I have been looking.

**The sheet.** Five rows, one per anchor — Earth, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune. Each row: the real photograph on the left, the computed swatch on the right, and three columns for students to complete: **Agree / Disagree**, **How different (a word, not a number)**, **What could explain it**. Nothing else on the page. The photograph credits (Apollo 17, Cassini ×2, Voyager 2 ×2) go in small print — they matter in the discussion.

**Run of the lesson**

- **0–5 · Hook, no explanation.** Show the five photographs and the five swatches shuffled. "Match them." They will get four instantly and argue about Neptune and Uranus. Do not resolve it.
- **5–12 · Individual, silent, on paper.** Fill the Agree/Disagree column for all five. Silent and individual matters: it forces a commitment before peer pressure, and it gives me a page of evidence from every student, including the ones who never speak.
- **12–22 · Pairs.** Complete "what could explain it" for the two rows they disagreed on. Prompt on the board, unchanged all lesson: **"If the model and the photo disagree, list every thing that could be at fault."** Expect: model's wrong; camera's wrong; picture's been edited; planet changed; they're not showing the same thing.
- **22–32 · The reveal, and it must be in this order.** First tell them the computed colour comes from a **measured** 1995 spectrum of Neptune, put through the same maths as all 5,700 exoplanets on the site. Then tell them Voyager's blue was contrast-enhanced. Give it ten seconds of silence. Then Uranus — a genuine, unenhanced photograph the model matches well — so they cannot generalise to "photos always lie".
- **32–40 · The write-up.** See below. Collect it.

**What they write down** — one paragraph, structured, on the sheet:

> **Claim:** the model and the photograph of Neptune disagree because ______.
> **Evidence:** I know this because ______.
> **Reasoning:** this means that when a model disagrees with an observation, you should first check ______.

**Where the discussion has to land.** Not "the model was right, well done" — that inverts the misconception instead of removing it. It lands on: **a disagreement is a question about the pipeline, not a verdict on either side.** Both the model and the photograph have processing steps in them. The photograph has *more* than most students think, and the model has *fewer*. The trustworthy move is to ask what each one did to the light before it reached your eye. If a student says "so how do we know the exoplanet colours are right?" — that is the best question in the lesson, and the honest answer is "we don't, and that is exactly why they checked it against five planets we have photographs of."

**Assessment, which is the part that decides whether it gets used twice.** Mark the CER paragraph out of 3:

- **1 —** identifies a disagreement and gives any explanation (usually "the model is wrong").
- **2 —** identifies that the photograph could have been altered, or that model and photo may not show the same thing.
- **3 —** states that both are processed, and names a *check* that would settle it — a second photograph, another instrument, the original data.

A class distribution across those three bands is a genuinely useful piece of evidence for a department's "working scientifically" tracking, and it takes eight minutes to mark thirty. **Exit ticket** (30 seconds, for the ones who finish early or for a cover lesson): *"A model disagrees with a measurement. Name one thing you would check first."*

**Wifi-down version.** Identical. Print the sheet with the images on it. The lesson loses nothing — which is the single strongest argument for building this one and not the click-driven worksheet.

**Extension, no prep:** the site flags microlensing planets as model-only because no light is ever received from them. "Should those be on the site at all?" is a five-minute argument that lands the same objective from the other side.

**What the maintainer has to produce:** one A4 student sheet, one A4 teacher note (misconception targeted, the Neptune fact with its citation, the 3-band mark scheme, three likely wrong answers and what to say). Two pages. That is the whole thing.

## As a planetarium: what we'd actually take

*Education officer speaking.* The doc's fulldome verdict is right and I would go further: **do not produce a fulldome clip either**, not even 60 seconds, until someone has asked for one. Fulldome masters are 4K×4K fisheye at minimum, most domes want 8K, and a clip that is slightly wrong geometrically is unusable rather than imperfect. Rank 3 in the doc is still one rank too high.

What we would take, honestly:

1. **A single still image, at dome resolution, that we can drop into a live show.** Not a loop, not a video — one picture of the 5,700-swatch wall. Our presenters improvise nightly and a striking still with two sentences of script is the thing that actually gets used, for years. Cost to you: an export. This is the minimum viable version and it is smaller than anything the doc proposes.
2. **The lobby screen loop — yes, but on our terms.** We do have idle screens. What we cannot take is a web page: our foyer machines are locked, often offline, and run whatever the AV contractor supports. Ship a **plain MP4, 1920×1080, no audio, no text smaller than 40px, silent-legible, under 3 minutes, looping cleanly.** A kiosk URL will be politely accepted and never installed.
3. **The presenter's aside.** Two sentences and a URL, as the doc says. Correct.

The doc's "email three local science centres" is right, and the Pavilhão do Conhecimento double-door (ESERO + science centre in one building) is genuinely the best single move in this document. But lead with the still image, not the loop — asking a museum to install video is a procurement conversation; giving a presenter a picture is a favour.

## Gaps

- **No answer key exists for anything.** The doc names the teacher note as "the artefact hobby projects never make" and then does not schedule one. It is the difference between adoption and a download.
- **No accessibility line.** A colour-based resource needs a colour-vision statement. Roughly one boy in twelve in my class is colour-deficient, and a lesson about *hue* will fail them silently unless the sheet also names the colour in words. Say so on the artefact; it is also a strong honesty note.
- **No cover-lesson version.** The single most-shared resource type in any school is the one a non-specialist can run cold. A one-page version with the answers on the teacher sheet would circulate further than everything else combined.
- **Nothing on safeguarding/GDPR-shaped questions.** Portuguese and UK schools will ask what the site collects. One sentence — "no accounts, no cookies, cookieless analytics only" — pre-empts an IT block.
- **No named class size or lab requirement.** Trivial, and its absence signals "written by an enthusiast".

The 10.º-ano AE domains are *Elementos químicos e sua organização*, *Propriedades e transformações da matéria*, *Energia e sua conservação*; the subdomain is *Energia dos eletrões nos átomos*. Verified from the DGE PDF (`10_fq_a.pdf`, Agosto 2018). **Corrected, with verbatim quotable outcomes — including one that names identifying elements in stars.**

- **The best Portuguese hook was missed entirely:** 11.º ano, *Ondas e eletromagnetismo* — "…no conhecimento do Universo, integrando aspetos que evidenciem o **carácter provisório do conhecimento científico**". **Added.**

- **The five anchors are Earth, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune — not Mars.** Verified in `pipeline/solar_system.py`. **Corrected.**
- **Astronomy on Tap: "remote is the ask" is bad advice.** The site says presenters are typically from local research and educational institutions and makes no mention of virtual events; the streaming chapters were a 2020 arrangement. **Corrected, including the draft email.**

- **Still unverified (403s this pass):** OER Commons Open Author terms, NASA's Universe of Learning current operating status, the IPS free-materials page, and whether Night Sky Notes is still commissioning. ## Better approaches

1. **Build the anchor lesson and nothing else this term.** Two A4 pages. It is the only artefact that survives a wifi failure, produces markable evidence, and is genuinely scarce. One excellent resource beats six adequate ones by a wide margin here, because a teacher adopts *a lesson*, not a website — and having adopted one, they will explore the site on their own time.
2. **`/teach` as a one-page landing for exactly that lesson.** Not a hub for six things that don't exist. Licence, no-login, download, time box, contact.
3. **Submit that one lesson to astroEDU.** It is peer-reviewed, CC BY, and this activity is unusually well matched to what they publish. Slow; start it and forget it.
4. **Walk into the Pavilhão do Conhecimento with the Portuguese version printed.** Not an email with a link — a printed sheet, in Portuguese, with the 11.º-ano AE sentence quoted at the top. That is a fifteen-minute conversation that no online listing will ever match.
5. **The A3 poster**, because it is nearly free once the render code exists and it sits on a wall for a decade.
6. **Everything else** — the second worksheet, the 20-planet pack, the fulldome clip, TES, OER Commons, Night Sky Network — only after a teacher who is not you has run the lesson and said something.

## The one thing I'd change

Cut the build list from seven items to two — the anchor lesson and the `/teach` page that serves it — and spend the reclaimed weekends on the teacher note and the mark scheme instead. A resource that generates evidence of learning gets used every September; one that doesn't gets used once.
