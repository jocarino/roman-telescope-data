# Wikimedia Commons — the honest verdict

**Status:** **parked** · **Effort:** medium · **Payoff:** low–medium, slower and riskier than it looks · **Hub:** [Marketing plan](./README.md)

You asked how worth this actually is. Short answer: **less than it looks, and not yet.** Here
is the reasoning, plus the condition that would flip it.

## Why it looked good

Hundreds of exoplanet articles on Wikipedia have no image, or use a NASA artist's impression.
Those articles get steady traffic forever. An image placed there is permanent, algorithm-proof
referral, and uploading is free. On paper it's the best-value idea in the whole plan.

## Why it isn't

**1. Wikipedia will treat our colours as original research, and it will be right to.**
Wikipedia's core content policies exclude unpublished analysis. A colour computed by one
person's pipeline — however sound the physics — is exactly that until it appears in a
peer-reviewed source or is covered by a reliable secondary one. A NASA artist's impression is
admissible because NASA is a citable publisher; we are not, yet. An editor removing our image
would be applying the policy correctly, not being unfair.

**2. Adding your own work to articles is a conflict of interest.** Uploading to Commons is
fine. *Placing your own images into articles* is what the COI guidance asks you not to do, and
doing it at scale across hundreds of exoplanet articles would read as exactly what it is:
a promotion campaign. That's a reputational risk to a project whose entire selling point is
honesty. Not worth it.

**3. The traffic return is worse than assumed.** Commons attribution lives on the *file
description page*, not in the article. A reader sees the image; they do not see a clickable
link to us unless they click through to the file page first, which almost nobody does. The
realistic referral is very small — this is a credibility play dressed as a traffic play, and
the credibility isn't there yet either (see 1).

**4. Mass uploads have their own bureaucracy.** Uploading a handful is trivial; uploading
thousands means a bot flag, a community discussion, and a maintenance obligation when the data
changes. That's a weekend spent on process.

**Net:** a real chance of spending several evenings, getting reverted, and looking like a
self-promoter — in exchange for traffic that other items in this plan produce in an afternoon.
[09-show-hn.md](./09-show-hn.md), [13-credit-the-scientists.md](./13-credit-the-scientists.md)
and [03-seo-planet-pages.md](./03-seo-planet-pages.md) all beat it comfortably.

## What would flip it

The blocker is sourceability, and it is fixable — deliberately, in this order:

1. **Publish the dataset with a DOI.** A Zenodo record makes the colours a *citable published
   source* rather than a website's claim. See [06-open-data.md](./06-open-data.md). This is the
   single change that moves the whole thing from inadmissible to arguable.
2. **Get one piece of third-party coverage** — a science outlet, an AAS Nova write-up, or a
   scientist's own blog. See [13-credit-the-scientists.md](./13-credit-the-scientists.md) and
   [15-roman-launch.md](./15-roman-launch.md). That's the secondary source an editor needs.
3. **Then don't add anything yourself.** Upload the renders to Commons under CC BY-SA, and
   raise it on the relevant WikiProject Astronomy talk page: *"I've published this dataset with
   a DOI, the images are on Commons, editors are welcome to use them if they think they're
   appropriate."* Let editors decide. If they use them, it's legitimate and durable. If they
   don't, you lost twenty minutes rather than a month.

That sequence turns a slightly grubby tactic into an honest contribution, which is the only
version of this that fits the project.

## The small version that's worth doing anyway

Independent of articles: **upload a dozen of the best renders to Commons under CC BY-SA now**,
with good file descriptions explaining the method and linking the source. Cost: one evening.
It makes the images legitimately reusable by anyone — teachers
([14-educators.md](./14-educators.md)), bloggers, journalists — without them having to ask.
Don't expect traffic from it; treat it as removing friction for other people's reuse.

## How we'll know it worked

Only relevant if it ever gets un-parked. The measure would be article placements that *someone
else* made, and Commons file-page views. Not worth instrumenting until then.

## Risks

- Being seen as a self-promoter in a community with long memories, at a stage where our
  credibility is the whole asset.
- Sinking evenings into process rather than the channels that actually move.
